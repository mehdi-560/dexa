Reefer freight typically pays more per mile than dry van, but it also costs more to operate — the reefer unit burns fuel, requires its own maintenance, and demands tighter scheduling. Whether reefer nets more than dry van depends on your routes, your discipline with scheduling, and how well your costs are managed. Many carriers find reefer worth it; others prefer the simplicity of dry van. This comparison gives you the real picture on both.

## What dry van hauls and what it costs

Dry van is the most common equipment in trucking — a 53-foot enclosed trailer pulling general freight: retail goods, packaged food, paper, furniture, consumer products. Almost any shipper has dry van freight, which means more load options and more competition.

**Costs to run dry van:**
- Trailer purchase or lease (or trailer pool if power-only)
- Maintenance on the trailer itself (lighter than a reefer unit)
- Standard fuel for the tractor

Dry van is simpler to operate. No temperature management, no reefer maintenance, no fuel for a refrigeration unit. It's the right equipment for carriers who want the widest freight access and the lowest equipment complexity.

**The trade-off:** because it's the most common equipment, rates are the most competitive. You're competing with more trucks for the same loads.

## What reefer hauls and what it costs

A reefer (refrigerated trailer) hauls temperature-sensitive freight: produce, dairy, meat, poultry, frozen foods, some pharmaceuticals. The reefer unit on the trailer keeps cargo within a specified temperature range throughout the trip.

**Additional costs compared to dry van:**
- Fuel for the reefer unit, running independently of the tractor
- Reefer unit maintenance and repair — a real cost that can be significant
- Pre-cooling time before loading
- Stricter scheduling requirements (temperature compliance and appointment windows)

Reefer rates typically run above dry van for the same lane, compensating for the extra cost and complexity. During regional produce seasons — California's Central Valley harvests, Texas winter produce, Florida citrus — rates can spike meaningfully.

## Which pays more after costs?

This is the honest question, and the answer is: it depends on how well you manage the extra costs. Reefer's higher gross rate can be partly or fully consumed by the reefer unit's fuel burn and maintenance if those aren't managed well.

Carriers who do well on reefer tend to:
- Plan loads around produce seasons to catch rate spikes
- Keep the reefer unit serviced to avoid expensive breakdowns
- Negotiate detention clearly upfront, since tight appointment windows mean longer waits

Carriers who struggle on reefer tend to under-account for unit fuel and maintenance costs, treating their reefer rate as if it were the same as a dry van rate.

## What about produce seasons?

Produce seasons create real rate spikes for reefer. The California Central Valley (Fresno, Salinas, Bakersfield) generates heavy outbound reefer from spring through fall. Florida's winter produce season, Texas's Rio Grande Valley produce, and the Pacific Northwest apple harvest in Washington are all periods when reefer demand and rates increase. A good dispatcher plans around these lanes to catch the peaks.

## The simple decision framework

**Choose reefer if:** you want higher rates and are willing to manage the extra costs and scheduling discipline, and you understand reefer maintenance costs before you commit.

**Choose dry van if:** you want the widest load availability, simpler operations, and don't want to manage a reefer unit.

Both equipment types are worth dispatching well. The rates and costs both shift by lane, season, and market, which is why a good dispatcher earns their fee — by keeping you in the right lanes at the right time regardless of your equipment.
