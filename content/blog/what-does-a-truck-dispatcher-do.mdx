A truck dispatcher finds and books freight for owner-operators and small fleets, negotiates the rate with brokers, and handles the paperwork — so the carrier can spend their time driving instead of searching load boards. In short, a dispatcher is the carrier's booking and back-office partner, usually paid a percentage of each load.

## What a truck dispatcher actually does

A dispatcher's day-to-day work for a carrier includes:

- **Finding loads** on load boards and through broker relationships that fit the carrier's equipment, location, and preferred lanes.
- **Negotiating the rate** with the broker to get the best possible pay for each load.
- **Booking the load** and handling the rate confirmation.
- **Setting up carrier packets** and paperwork with brokers so the carrier gets paid.
- **Planning routes and reloads** to keep the truck moving and reduce empty (deadhead) miles.
- **Handling problems** — detention, layovers, and schedule changes — while the driver focuses on the road.

## What a dispatcher does not do

A dispatcher is not a freight broker and does not own the freight. They work **on behalf of the carrier**, not the shipper. A good dispatcher's interests are aligned with the carrier's: when they book better-paying loads, the carrier earns more and so does the dispatcher.

## How much does a dispatcher cost?

Most dispatchers charge a percentage of each load, typically 5–10%, or a flat weekly fee. The percentage model keeps the dispatcher motivated to negotiate higher rates, since they earn more only when the carrier does. [See how Dexent's pricing works](/pricing).

## Do you need a dispatcher?

If you're spending hours on load boards, leaving money on the table in negotiations, or drowning in broker paperwork, a dispatcher usually pays for itself — the higher rates and saved time typically outweigh the fee. New authorities especially benefit, since dispatchers bring broker relationships a new carrier hasn't built yet.
