A percentage dispatch fee means you pay a share of each load's gross rate, so the dispatcher earns more when they book better-paying freight. A flat weekly fee means you pay the same amount regardless of what you haul. Which is better depends on your weekly gross: at higher volumes, flat fees often cost less; at lower or uneven volumes, a percentage can be more predictable. The real question is not which model sounds better but which one leaves more money in your pocket at your actual numbers.

## How percentage-based dispatch works

Under a percentage model, you pay a share of the gross rate on each load — typically somewhere between 5% and 10%, though both lower and higher rates exist. If you run a $2,500 load at 8%, the dispatcher takes $200 and you keep $2,300 before fuel and other costs.

The main advantage is alignment. Because the dispatcher's pay rises when yours does, they have a direct incentive to negotiate higher rates. If they book a cheap load, they earn less too. This model also adjusts naturally to slow weeks — if you don't run, you don't pay.

The main drawback is that the fee scales with rates. During peak freight seasons when rates are high, you pay more in absolute dollars even though the dispatcher's work hasn't necessarily changed. Some carriers find that uncomfortable.

## How flat-fee dispatch works

Under a flat-fee model, you pay a set amount per week regardless of how many loads you run or what they pay. Some services publish flat weekly rates. For example, O Trucking lists $250 per week for semis on its website.

The main advantage is cost predictability. You know exactly what dispatch costs each week, which makes budgeting easier. At high weekly gross, a flat fee often costs less than a percentage — a carrier grossing $5,000 a week pays $250 flat instead of $400 at 8%.

The main drawback is incentive. A dispatcher earning a flat fee gets paid the same whether they book you a great load or a mediocre one. Some flat-fee services maintain high quality regardless, but the structural incentive is weaker than a percentage model.

## The break-even math

The break-even point between a percentage and a flat fee is easy to calculate. Divide the flat weekly fee by the percentage to get the weekly gross at which they're equal.

For example, $250 flat versus 8%: $250 ÷ 0.08 = $3,125. At a weekly gross below $3,125, the flat fee costs more. Above $3,125, it costs less.

Work out that number for any comparison you're making, then honestly assess what your average weekly gross is — including slow weeks, not just your best ones.

## Which model suits which carrier?

**Percentage tends to suit:** carriers with variable weekly revenue, new authorities still building consistency, and carriers who want the dispatcher's incentive aligned with their earnings.

**Flat fee tends to suit:** carriers running steady high volume who want to cap their dispatch cost, and carriers who have strong broker relationships of their own and mainly need load coverage and paperwork support.

## What about hybrid models?

Some services use a lower percentage plus a small monthly base fee. This gives the dispatcher a floor of income while keeping some incentive alignment. It can work well, but make sure you're comparing the total cost — base fee plus percentage — against a pure-percentage or pure-flat alternative.
