Truck dispatchers are paid by the carrier they work for, not by the broker or shipper. The two common models are a percentage of each load's gross rate — usually somewhere between 5% and 10% — or a flat weekly fee per truck. With a percentage model, the dispatcher only earns more when they book you higher-paying loads, which keeps their incentive aligned with yours.

## The percentage model

Under a percentage model, the dispatcher takes an agreed cut of each load's gross rate. If you run a $2,000 load at 8%, the dispatcher earns $160 and you keep the rest. The advantage for the carrier is alignment: because the dispatcher's pay rises and falls with your rates, they're motivated to negotiate hard and keep you on better freight. When you earn more, they earn more.

## The flat-fee model

Under a flat-fee model, you pay a set amount per truck per week regardless of how much you haul or what the loads pay. This gives predictable costs, and on a high-revenue week it can work out cheaper than a percentage. The downside is that the dispatcher earns the same whether they find you a cheap load or a great one — so the incentive to push for higher rates is weaker.

## Which is better for owner-operators?

Neither is automatically better — it depends on your volume and the dispatcher's skill. But for most owner-operators, a percentage model works in their favor precisely because it ties the dispatcher's paycheck to your success. The key is judging the total picture: what you actually net per mile *after* the fee, not the fee percentage in isolation. A dispatcher charging a bit more but booking much better loads leaves you ahead.

## When does the dispatcher get paid?

Payment terms vary. Some dispatchers bill weekly for the loads booked that week; some are tied to when you get paid. A transparent dispatcher will tell you clearly and in advance exactly how and when their fee is charged — if the answer is vague, that's a red flag.
