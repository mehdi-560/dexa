Most truck dispatchers charge **5–10% of each load**, or a flat weekly fee. Which one is right depends on how much you run and how well the dispatcher negotiates. Here's how the numbers actually work.

## What's a typical dispatcher fee?

The most common model is a **percentage of the load's gross rate**. For owner-operators, that's usually in the 5–10% range, with most settling around 6–8%. Some dispatchers instead charge a **flat weekly rate** — often $150–$300 per truck per week.

Neither is automatically cheaper. A percentage scales with your revenue; a flat fee doesn't. On a strong week a flat fee can look like a bargain, but it removes the dispatcher's incentive to fight for higher rates.

## Percentage vs flat fee — which is better?

A percentage keeps the dispatcher's interest aligned with yours: they only earn more when they book you better-paying loads. A flat fee gets you predictability, but the dispatcher earns the same whether they find you a $2.00/mile load or a $3.00/mile one.

If your dispatcher is good at negotiating, a percentage usually pays for itself — because the higher rate they secure more than covers the fee.

## The number that actually matters

Don't compare dispatchers on fee alone. Compare them on **your net-per-mile after the fee**. A 5% dispatcher who books cheap freight can leave you with less than an 8% dispatcher who negotiates hard.

For example: 5% on a $2.00/mile load nets you $1.90/mile. 8% on a $2.80/mile load nets you $2.58/mile — even though the fee percentage is higher.

## What should you watch out for?

Hidden fees, setup charges, and long-term contracts are the red flags. A fair dispatcher states one price, has no surprise add-ons, and doesn't lock you in.
