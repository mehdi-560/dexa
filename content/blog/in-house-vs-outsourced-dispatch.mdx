In-house dispatch means hiring your own dispatcher as an employee; outsourced dispatch means paying a service a percentage or flat fee per truck to find and book loads for you. In-house gives you a dedicated person who knows your operation deeply; outsourced is more flexible and requires less management, and whether it costs less depends on your volume. For most owner-operators and small fleets, outsourced dispatch is the more practical starting point. Larger fleets with steady volume and a back office to support an employee often find in-house dispatch worth the investment.

## What in-house dispatch involves

An in-house dispatcher is your employee. They work full-time, search load boards, negotiate with brokers, handle rate confirmations and paperwork, and coordinate directly with your drivers. For a fleet with enough trucks running every day, one dispatcher can keep everyone loaded.

The costs include salary, payroll taxes, benefits, and the time you spend managing another employee. A full-time dispatcher's salary varies by location and experience. Beyond pay, you're also responsible for training them, covering their sick days, and replacing them if they leave.

The benefit is dedication. An employee who knows your trucks, your drivers, your preferred lanes, and your relationships with brokers becomes increasingly valuable over time. They are fully focused on your operation, not dividing attention across multiple carrier clients.

## What outsourced dispatch involves

An outsourced dispatcher or dispatch service works across multiple carrier clients. You pay a percentage of each load or a flat weekly fee per truck. They find loads, negotiate rates, and handle paperwork, but they work with other carriers too — your trucks are one account among many.

The costs scale with your freight instead of being fixed. You don't manage an employee, pay benefits, or worry about covering someone's absence. Under a percentage model, a slow week means a smaller fee.

The trade-off is depth. An outsourced service may never learn your lanes as deeply as a dedicated employee, and you're one of several clients, not the only focus.

## How do the costs compare?

Start with the fee math. Take the in-house dispatcher's total yearly cost (salary, payroll taxes, benefits), divide by 12, then divide by your outsourced fee percentage. The result is the monthly gross at which the two cost the same.

Here is an illustration with made-up numbers, not a market average. If an in-house dispatcher cost $60,000 a year, that's $5,000 a month. At an 8% fee, $5,000 divided by 0.08 is $62,500 in monthly gross freight. Above that, the fee alone can favor in-house.

But fees are only part of the picture. A single dispatcher can only manage so many trucks at once, and in-house comes with extra costs and risks: load board subscriptions and software, training time, covering absences, and the chance of losing someone you've trained. So the real decision is about capacity, quality, and control, not just the fee.

## When to consider moving in-house

- You have enough steady freight to keep a full-time dispatcher busy
- You have the management capacity to hire, train, and supervise a dispatcher
- Your lanes are established enough that a dedicated person can build deep relationships
- You want full operational control and are building a long-term operation

## When outsourced dispatch makes more sense

- You run a small number of trucks
- Your volume is variable or seasonal
- You want flexibility without employee overhead
- You're a new authority still establishing your lanes
- You'd rather focus on driving and operations than managing staff

## A middle path: outsourced now, in-house later

Many carriers start outsourced, grow their operation, build their lanes, and eventually hire in-house once the volume justifies it. That's a reasonable progression. Using outsourced dispatch while growing doesn't prevent you from switching later; it just reduces risk while you're still building.
